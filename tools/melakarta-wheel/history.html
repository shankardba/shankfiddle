History & Theory of the Mēḷakarta System

How Carnatic music arrived at exactly 72 parent scales, how each one got its name, and how the whole system falls out of a small set of rules rather than 72 unrelated facts to memorize.

What problem this solves

A rāga in Carnatic (South Indian classical) music is much more than a scale — it carries characteristic phrases, emphasis, and mood. But underneath every rāga's melodic personality is a simpler question: which seven pitches, out of twelve possible semitones, does it use? A Mēḷakarta is a rāga that answers that question in the most complete, unadorned way possible — all seven pitches, ascending and descending, with nothing skipped and nothing added. Musicians use the 72 mēḷakartas as a reference grid: any other rāga (a janya, "derived" rāga) can be located relative to one of these 72 parents.

The rules that make a scale a Mēḷakarta

Three conditions, all sourced from the standard definition:

  1. Sampūrṇa — all seven svaras (scale degrees) appear in both the ascending (Ārōhaṇam) and descending (Avarōhaṇam) scale.
  2. The upper octave Ṡa is included — a scale that stops short of the octave (like Punnāgavarāḷi or Chenchuruṭṭi) doesn't qualify.
  3. Ascending and descending use the same seven notes — no zig-zag scales.

The twelve semitones and where each svara can sit

Carnatic theory divides the octave into twelve positions, but names them by function rather than by fixed pitch the way a piano's white/black keys are: Sa and Pa (the 1st and 5th) never move. Ma has two positions. Ri, Ga, Dha and Ni each have three — and here's the part that actually generates the number 72: two of those three positions for Ri/Ga and two of the three for Dha/Ni are shared with their neighbor.

Position 2 is both R₂ and G₁ — the same physical pitch, wearing two different names depending on which role it's playing. Same for position 3 (R₃/G₂) and positions 9 and 10 on the Dha/Ni side. This is exactly why a scale can't just pick any Ri and any Ga independently: if it picked R₂ and G₁, that would be the same note claiming two roles in one scale — not a valid seven-note scale at all.

The rule, precisely: a valid Ri/Ga pair needs Ri's index ≤ Ga's index (R₁G₁, R₁G₂, R₁G₃, R₂G₂, R₂G₃, R₃G₃ — six combinations, not nine). The identical rule governs Dha/Ni. Ma has no such constraint — M₁ and M₂ are genuinely separate pitches (positions 5 and 6), which is why Ma contributes a clean ×2 rather than folding into the Ri/Ga-style count.

Put together: 6 (Ri/Ga combinations) × 2 (Ma) × 6 (Dha/Ni combinations) = 72. Every mēḷakarta is one point in this 6×2×6 grid — which is also exactly how the wheel is laid out: the 12 chakras are the 6 Ri/Ga pairs doubled by the 2 Ma choices, and the 6 rāgas inside each chakra are the 6 Dha/Ni pairs.

A short history

The idea of organizing rāgas into a parent-scale scheme predates the current numbering. Rāmāmātya's Svaramēḷakalānidhi (c. 1550) is generally credited as the starting point. About a century later, the musicologist Veṅkaṭamakhin (17th century), in his Chaturdaṇḍī Prakāśikā, proposed a 72-rāga mēḷa system — arrived at by a somewhat idiosyncratic selection of Madhyama variants for the underlying scale-generation logic, which musicologists still debate. What later became the standard, widely-taught version — the one this wheel uses — was standardized by Govindāchārya, who is also credited with renaming several of Veṅkaṭamakhin's scales to the names in common use today.

A separate, earlier numbering scheme (the "asampūrṇa" or incomplete scheme, associated with the Muthuswāmi Dīkṣitar school) used some scales that aren't sampūrṇa in the modern sense — see Wikipedia's Melakarta (asampurna scheme) article. This wheel uses the Govindāchārya/standard numbering that all three cross-checked sources agree on.

The katapayādi system — reading a rāga's number off its own name

About a hundred years after Veṅkaṭamakhin, musicians began applying an older Sanskrit numeral-encoding convention — the katapayādi saṅkhya — to name each mēḷakarta so that its number could be read directly from its first two syllables, without a lookup table. Sanskrit consonants map to digits in four groups (ka-varga, ṭa-varga, pa-varga, ya-varga — hence "ka-ṭa-pa-ya"); the rule saṅkhyānām vāmatō gatiḥ ("for numbers, the movement is from the left" — i.e. read the digits right-to-left) then turns those two digits into the rāga's index.

Harikāmbhōji → first two syllables: Ha, ri Ha = 8 ri = 2 Write them in order encountered: 8, 2 Reverse (read right-to-left): 2, 8 → 28 Harikāmbhōji is Mēḷakarta #28. Check it on the wheel — Bāṇa chakra. ✓

The same trick works in reverse for building a name from scratch, which is how a new-numbered scale gets a pronounceable name rather than just "mēḷakarta 47." (Full digit-to-consonant table: Wikipedia's Katapayadi system article.)

The chakras — grouping by pattern, not just by number

The 72 rāgas split into 12 chakras ("wheels") of 6 each. Rāgas within one chakra share the same Ri, Ga and Ma — they differ only in Dha and Ni. Each chakra's name is a traditional counting-mnemonic (there are N of this thing in Hindu cosmology/ritual, so it's chakra N) — a second, independent way the number is encoded into the name, on top of katapayādi:

1 · Induthe moon — one
2 · Netraeyes — two
3 · Agnithree sacred fires
4 · Vēdafour Vedas
5 · Bāṇafive arrows of Manmatha
6 · Ṛtusix seasons
7 · Ṛṣiseven sages
8 · Vasueight Vasus
9 · Brahmanine (Brahma-related count)
10 · Diśiten directions
11 · Rudraeleven Rudras
12 · Ādityatwelve Ādityas

Chakras 1–6 use Śuddha Madhyama (M₁); chakras 7–12 use Prati Madhyama (M₂), each an exact copy of the same six Ri/Ga groupings one Ma-step higher. On the wheel, that's the right half versus the left half.

Comparison: Carnatic mēḷakarta vs. Hindustani thāṭ

Hindustani (North Indian) classical music has a broadly analogous idea — the thāṭ, a parent scale that groups rāgas by shared scale-degree content — but the systems aren't a clean 1:1 match:

Mēḷakarta (Carnatic)Thāṭ (Hindustani)
72, generated exhaustively by the R≤G / D≤N combinatorics above10, a curated/practical set — not derived from an exhaustive combinatorial rule
Every one of the 72 is playable and named, whether or not it's musically common in practiceThe 10 thāṭs were chosen to cover the rāgas actually in common use, not to be exhaustive
Katapayādi naming encodes the number in the name itselfThāṭ names are typically named after their best-known representative rāga

In short: mēḷakarta is "generate every mathematically possible complete scale, then name and number all of them," while thāṭ is "classify the rāgas that already exist into a manageable number of families." Different design goals, not competing answers to the same question.

Case study: building the (Western) altered scale the mēḷakarta way

The combinatorial grid above isn't just a way to catalog 72 existing rāgas — it's a small enough rule-set that you can hand it any scale from another tradition and ask, structurally, whether the mēḷakarta system could have produced it too. A good test case: jazz's altered scale (a.k.a. super Locrian) — 1 ♭2 ♯2 3 ♭5 ♯5 ♭7, the scale most commonly played over a dominant chord with every alterable tension raised or lowered.

Why it can never be one of the 72, rooted at its own tonic

Section 3 above established the rule: Sa and Pa never move — every one of the 72 mēḷakartas keeps a perfect 5th (position 7) fixed above its own Sa, no exceptions, because Pa isn't one of the combinatorial choices at all. The altered scale's entire identity, though, is built on flattening that exact degree — ♭5 is right there in its name. A scale that moves the one degree the mēḷakarta grid holds structurally fixed cannot itself be a point on that grid. This isn't a numbering gap or an omission — it's disqualified by the same rule that generates all 72 in the first place.

But it's already inside the system — reached by rotation, not by root

Carnatic theory has its own native tool for exactly this situation: Graha Bhēdam — shifting which svara of an existing scale you treat as the new Sa, keeping every pitch fixed. It's the same operation Western theory calls "modes of a scale," just framed around a movable drone reference instead of a movable scale-degree label. Run it on Gourimanōhari (mēḷakarta #23 — R₂ G₂ M₁ P D₂ N₃), and something exact falls out:

Gourimanōhari (#23), ascending: S R₂ G₂ M₁ P D₂ N₃ Ṡ Semitones from its own Sa: 0, 2, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12 → this is *exactly* the Western ascending melodic minor scale. Now shift the reference point to Gourimanōhari's own Ni (semitone 11) — Graha Bhēdam, not a new rāga, same seven pitches: Ni → Ṡ : 12 − 11 = 1 (half step) Ṡ → Ri : 14 − 12 = 2 (whole step) Ri → Ga : 15 − 14 = 1 (half step) Ga → Ma : 17 − 15 = 2 (whole step) Ma → Pa : 19 − 17 = 2 (whole step) Pa → Dha: 21 − 19 = 2 (whole step) Dha → Ni: 23 − 21 = 2 (whole step) Step pattern: 1 2 1 2 2 2 2 — the altered scale's step pattern, exactly.
So the altered scale isn't outside the mēḷakarta system after all — it's what Gourimanōhari already sounds like, starting from Ni instead of Sa. This is also exactly how jazz theory itself defines the altered scale (the 7th mode of melodic minor) — the two traditions arrive at the identical fact through unrelated formal routes: Carnatic combinatorics (R≤G, D≤N, Pa fixed) on one side, stacked-diatonic modal numbering on the other.

Not the only door in

Gourimanōhari's seven pitches are common enough elsewhere in the grid that three other mēḷakartas — each already named, each valid entirely on its own terms — turn out to be that same seven-pitch collection, just already rooted at a different one of its degrees:

MēḷakartaFormulaRooted at Gourimanōhari's…Note
Nāṭakapriyā (#10)R₁ G₂ M₁ P D₂ N₂Ri—
Chārukēśi (#26)R₂ G₃ M₁ P D₁ N₂Ga—
Vāchaspati (#64)R₂ G₃ M₂ P D₂ N₂Mathe Carnatic near-equivalent of jazz's Lydian dominant — melodic minor's own 4th mode

Each works as a standalone rooted mēḷakarta (Pa intact, R≤G and D≤N both satisfied) precisely because a perfect 5th above that particular degree also happens to already sit inside Gourimanōhari's seven pitches. Ni is the one degree of the seven where that isn't true — which is the structural reason the altered scale can only be reached by Graha Bhēdam, never by its own root.

Try it on the wheel: open Gourimanōhari and compare its Ārōhaṇam to the Western melodic minor scale note-for-note. The mēḷakarta grid didn't need to know jazz theory existed to already contain it.

Real-world grounding

Even though all 72 are valid and numbered, only a fraction are in wide performance use — rāgas like Māyāmāḷavagowḷa (#15, often a student's very first scale), Kharaharapriyā (#22), Harikāmbhōji (#28), Dhīraśankarābharaṇam (#29 — the Carnatic equivalent of the Western major scale) and Mēchakalyāni (#65 — equivalent to the Lydian mode) anchor a huge share of the standard repertoire. Most of the other ~65 rāgas are used far less often in concert but remain the source scales for countless janya rāgas that omit or reorder notes from them (Wikipedia's Hindōḷam-from-Naṭabhairavi/Hanumatōdi example is the classic illustration of how a janya can plausibly trace to more than one parent).

A historical numbering slip, corrected

Worth knowing if you ever cross-reference an older chart: an early published diagram of the wheel had rāgas 27/28 (Sarasāngi/Harikāmbhōji) and 70/71 (Nāsikābhūṣaṇi/Kōsalam) swapped. The standard numbering used today — and used throughout this wheel — has the corrected order, and it's what all three sources this app was cross-checked against (Wikipedia's table, karnatik.com's chart, and melakarta.com) already agree on.

Try it on the wheel: jump to Harikāmbhōji and check its formula against the worked katapayādi example above (S R₂ G₃ M₁ P D₂ N₂ Ṡ, chakra 5 = Bāṇa). Or pick any two rāgas in the same chakra and compare — their Ri/Ga/Ma will match exactly, and only Dha/Ni will differ, exactly as the chakra rule predicts.
